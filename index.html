EU POLITICIANS’ HABIT OF SKIPPING WORK ON THURSDAYS MAY HAVE COST EUROPEANS THEIR PRIVACY

EU legislators like skipping work on Thursdays to catch the early train back home. That habit may now have contributed to passing the controversial “Chat Control” law, allowing major tech companies to scan EU citizens’ private messages without any suspicion of crime.

This analysis of years of EU voting data shows how. Further investigation also finds that those messages may end up in controversial AI company Palantir's possession.

By Arvid Grange
Published August 3, 2026

The votes were cast just before 13.30 on July 9th this year, a Thursday. The result: a majority of 314 vs 276 of the EU Parliament’s legislators voted to reject the law – the controversial ePrivacy Directive or, as it is more commonly known, Chat Control 1.0.

The law allows tech companies to scan EU citizens’ private messages with the aim of finding and combatting child sexual abuse material. In fact, the law had already been doing so since 2021 but expired this spring. The vote intended to reinstate the law or discard it.

Despite a clear majority in favour of discarding the legislation, the law was passed and extended until 2028.

It was, to use EU Parliament terminology, a second reading vote. A law proposal put forth by the EU Council after they have rejected a verdict by the Parliament.

When the Council presents such a proposal a simple majority is not enough to stop it. Parliament needs to muster an absolute majority, meaning that they need more than 360 votes, regardless of how many MEPs are present in the chamber.

These are the rules of the European Union.

By analysing years of EU Parliament voting data we could map how this requirement, combined with EU legislators' well-known habit to skip work on Thursdays, may have cost European citizens their digital privacy, and passed it to the controversial AI-company Palantir.

These cases are very rare, with our analysis only finding 96 second reading votes over 12 years of weeklong monthly voting sessions. The overwhelmingly most common route is for the EU Council to accept the Parliament’s position after the first reading.

Even rarer are times when parliament manages to gather an absolute majority and reject the Council’s position. Only five of these votes resulted in the Council’s position being overturned.

The true rarity of absolute majority votes is made clearer by the fact that 28 of these 96 votes were held on July 9th, on extending "Chat Control 1.0".

Of these, two votes reached the required absolute majority to reject the council’s position. These were not votes on rejecting the law as a whole, however, but to make small amendments, denying tech companies the right to scan messages that are protected with end-to-end encryption.

The votes to reject the law entirely, two of them in the same sitting, both failed. The first despite a clear majority. The 314 votes in favour were still well short of the 361 votes needed. The second vote to reject the law saw many MEPs switching sides, with only 276 still in favour of rejecting the law.

The EU Council normally does not get much resistance during second reading votes. Of all these 96 votes, only five reached a simple majority, the normal requirement for winning a vote. All of these five were votes on extending “Chat Control 1.0”.

This was not the first time the EU Parliament and the Council has butted heads over the law. Back in March of this year a similar vote was held. The result was the same, with a majority of MEPs voting to reject an extension of the law. The EU Council rejected Parliament's verdict and put it to a second reading.

Supporters of “Chat Control” argue that the scanning of private messages without prior suspicion of crime is necessary to combat child sexual abuse. Opponents of the law counter that it constitutes an unacceptable invasion of privacy.

According to the constitution of the European Union “Everyone has the right to the protection of personal data concerning them.”

But at least two of the major lobbyists behind the law – child protection organizations Thorn and WeProtect Global Alliance, which have had lobby meetings for the law with commissioner Ylva Johansson, the creator of the law proposal, as well as with the president of the European Comission, Ursula von der Leyen – have strong links to American tech and AI company Palantir.

Thorn officially lists Palantir as one of their “Tech Task Force Members” and in 2021 Palantir made a blog post stating that they had joined WeProtect Global Alliance.

Besides these partnerships Palantir shares an even more direct link to scanning the private messages. The big tech companies, being predominantly American, are bound by U.S federal law to hand the data over to the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC).

The NCMEC, in turn, uses Palantir’s software for analysing the data.

For reference, Palantir’s controversial software partnerships include, but are not limited to, the Israeli Defence Force “in support of war-related missions”, as well as the U.S Immigration and Customs Enforcement agency (ICE).

How does the fact that this vote was held on a Thursday possibly affect the outcome?

Although Strasbourg is the official seat of the EU Parliament, business as usual is done in Brussels, Belgium. The MEPs only go to Strasbourg for voting sessions one week each month, Monday through Thursday.

Mondays are consumed by the 4.5-hour train ride between the cities, and no major votes are held. Over the subsequent voting days the 720-seat chamber is rarely full and attendance changes drastically.

Attendance peaks on Tuesdays, the first real day of voting. An average of 641 MEPs are in the plenary chamber.

On Wednesdays the attendance drops a little bit, to 632 attendees.

Then it plummets, from 632 on Wednesday to just 587 on Thursday. By this time many MEPs want to get home to Brussels before the evening, so they skip out.

This dropoff on Thursdays is reflected in the days with the overall worst attendance in the parliament, according to data from 2023 until present.

Of the 50 worst attended days, 23 were Thursdays.

But the real number of MEPs skipping out on the Thursday afternoon plenary sessions are even higher. While 587 MEPs on average are signed up for the sessions on Thursday mornings, after noon another 13 are gone, the voting records show.

This brings the actual Thursday afternoon average down to 574. That is 67 MEPs fewer than the average Tuesday.

An absolute majority is still possible for Parliament to achieve, but high absences make it a statistically more improbable event.

The vote on extending the ePrivacy Directive, or “Chat Control 1.0”, has already gotten much criticism for being held on the last plenary session before the EU Parliament goes on summer recess. The argument being that many MEPs had left for early vacation.

“Chat Control 1.0 passed the European Parliament — through the back door”, Euronews called it in an article shortly after the vote.

In actuality, the vote had a higher attendance than the average Thursday, with just over 600 MEPs being present. Although that still places it well below the average Tuesday attendance of 641.

Breaking down the second reading votes held between 2014 and 2026 by weekday, there is no remarkable pattern showing that it is less likely for Parliament to overturn the Council’s position on a Thursday than any other day.

The two votes that did reach absolute majority on a Thursday were held only this July, and up until then no Thursday vote had ever reached the absolute majority necessary.

However, the sample of votes is too small to draw any conclusions.

A clearer pattern showing the drastic effect that a Thursday can have on the outcome of a vote like this one emerges when looking at the past three and a half years of regular voting.

Even though regular votes only require simple majorities, analyzing how many of them reaches absolute majority numbers anyway show that Thursday votes have a much lower chance of achieving an absolute majority.

Reaching a larger than 360-vote majority gets increasingly more unlikely towards the end of a regular voting week, from 82% of regular votes reaching such numbers on Tuesdays to only 61% on Thursdays.
